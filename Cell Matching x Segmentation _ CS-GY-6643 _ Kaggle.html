Overview

Welcome to Project 2. In this project, you will work with paired images of the same mouse brain, one captured in-vivo and one ex-vivo. Your task is to segment the neurons in both images and match the corresponding cells across them. This type of cell matching is still commonly done manually, and the training data provided for this competition was annotated by hand.

The Problem

A neuroscientist watches a living mouse think.

Through a small glass window in the skull, a two-photon microscope records hundreds of neurons firing while the animal behaves: learning a task, seeing a stimulus, making a decision. This is in-vivo imaging. It tells you what each cell did.

But it cannot tell you what each cell is. To know that (which genes it expresses, which cell type it belongs to, where its axons project), the brain has to be removed, sliced, chemically stained and imaged again on a completely different microscope. This is ex-vivo imaging. It tells you what each cell is, but it is a dead, distorted, differently-lit version of the same tissue.

Neither measurement is useful without the other. The function you recorded in the living animal only becomes science when you can point to the same cell in the post-mortem image and read out its identity.

This requires matching each cell in the in-vivo image to the same cell in the ex-vivo image. Done by hand, the matching is slow and limits how many cells a study can include.

Your Goal

Automate it.

Given a pair of images from the same animal (one in-vivo, one ex-vivo), your model must produce three things:

  1. An instance segmentation of the in-vivo image. One mask per cell.
  2. An instance segmentation of the ex-vivo image. One mask per cell.
  3. Match cells across the two images. Output a correspondence list of pairs, each pairing an in-vivo cell with the ex-vivo cell that is the same neuron.

All three are scored, and they are not independent: a match is only credited if both of its cells were segmented correctly first.

The Challenge

The in-vivo and ex-vivo images can look very different, and no alignment between them is provided. They may differ in:

  • resolution
  • field of view
  • contrast
  • visible tissue area
  • number of detectable cells

The tissue also changes between the two imaging stages. It can shrink, deform, tear slightly, or be sectioned at a different angle. Because of this, a cell that is clear in one image may look faint, stretched, shifted, or may not appear in the other image at all. The challenge is therefore to handle these appearance and geometric differences while still identifying the same neurons across both images.

Description

The Domain

In-vivo images come from two-photon calcium imaging in the living mouse. Neurons usually appear as bright, roughly round cell bodies against a darker background, making them relatively easier to identify and segment.

Ex-vivo images come from fluorescence microscopy after the tissue has been removed and processed. These images are larger and more difficult to segment. Cell bodies can be surrounded by bright neuropil, many cells are faint, and some are only partially visible because of the sectioning process.

Another challenge is that the two images do not contain exactly the same set of cells. The in-vivo recording covers a volume, while each ex-vivo image is a thin section through that volume at an unknown depth and angle. As a result, some cells visible in one image may not appear in the other.

The Data

Five mice. Each mouse was imaged once in vivo, then cut into multiple ex-vivo sections. Every region in this dataset is one in-vivo image paired with one ex-vivo section from the same animal.

Technical Challenges

1. Most bright things in an ex-vivo image are not cells. Axons and dendrites glow about as brightly as cell bodies do. What makes a cell body a cell body is that it is compact, roughly round, and brighter than the pixels right around it. Brightness alone will not separate them.

2. The two images are at different scales. An ex-vivo image is 2-5x wider and taller than its in-vivo pair, but the cells are not 2-5x bigger. A cell covers far fewer pixels in the ex-vivo image. A detector tuned to one image will miss cells in the other.

3. Each cell needs its own mask, not a cell/background map. Labelling every pixel as "cell" or "background" is not enough. Two touching cells come out as one blob, and a blob cannot be matched to anything. Give every cell a separate mask and its own id.

Evaluation

Your submission is evaluated on three parts: in-vivo segmentation, ex-vivo segmentation, and cell matching. The three are then combined into one final score.

1. In-vivo segmentation: Panoptic Quality (PQ)

Your in-vivo masks are evaluated with Panoptic Quality. Each predicted mask is compared to the ground-truth masks by Intersection-over-Union, where |X| is the pixel count of X:

IoU(P,G)=|P∩G||P∪G|

A predicted mask is a true positive (TP) if its IoU with a ground-truth mask exceeds 0.75. Each ground-truth cell can be matched once. Unmatched predictions are false positives (FP) and unmatched ground-truth cells are false negatives (FN).

PQ=∑(p,g)∈TPIoU(p,g)|TP|+12|FP|+12|FN|

PQ is computed for each region, then averaged over regions.

2. Ex-vivo segmentation: Panoptic Quality (PQ)

Your ex-vivo masks are evaluated the same way, with the same 0.75 IoU threshold, giving a separate PQ for the ex-vivo images.

3. Cell matching: F1 score

Your match_pairs are evaluated with F1. A predicted pair [iv_id, ex_id] is correct if both masks are true positives and their ground-truth cells are a verified pair. Precision and recall are counted over all test regions together.

precision=correct predicted pairstotal predicted pairs,recall=correct predicted pairstotal verified pairs

F1=2⋅precision⋅recallprecision+recall

Each predicted cell may appear in at most one pair; otherwise the submission is rejected.

Final score

Segmentation and matching are weighted equally: the two PQs are averaged into one segmentation score, which counts for 0.5, and matching F1 counts for the other 0.5.

score=0.5⋅PQ(in-vivo)+PQ(ex-vivo)2+0.5⋅F1

Submission Format

1. What You Need to Submit

Submit one submission.csv file with one row per test region: 29 data rows plus a header.

The CSV must contain exactly these four columns, in this order:

sample_id,invivo_instances,exvivo_instances,match_pairs
  • sample_id: Copy directly from sample_submission.csv, without changing it.
  • invivo_instances: A JSON object mapping each predicted in-vivo cell ID to its mask as an RLE string.
  • exvivo_instances: A JSON object mapping each predicted ex-vivo cell ID to its mask as an RLE string.
  • match_pairs: A JSON list of [invivo_id, exvivo_id] pairs.

Predict all cells you detect, including cells without a match. Segmentation is scored on every cell.

Use {} for empty instance dictionaries and [] for no matches. Do not leave fields blank.

2. Run-Length Encoding

Encode each cell's binary mask separately using run-length encoding (RLE).

  • Flatten the mask in row-major order: left to right, then top to bottom (order="C").
  • Use zero-based indices. The top-left pixel has index 0.
  • Write space-separated integer pairs: start length start length ....
  • Each run starts at start and covers length consecutive foreground pixels.
  • Runs must be sorted in ascending order, must not overlap, and must stay within the image. Lengths must be positive.
  • An empty mask encodes as the empty string "".

Use the original image dimensions from invivo.tif or exvivo.tif. Dimensions are not stored in the RLE.

3. RLE Encoding and Decoding

import numpy as np


def encode_rle(mask: np.ndarray) -> str:
    """Convert a binary 2-D mask to zero-based, row-major RLE."""
    mask = np.asarray(mask, dtype=bool)
    if mask.ndim != 2:
        raise ValueError("mask must be a 2-D array")

    flat = mask.ravel(order="C")
    edges = np.flatnonzero(
        np.diff(np.concatenate(([False], flat, [False])))
    )
    return " ".join(
        f"{start} {end - start}"
        for start, end in edges.reshape(-1, 2)
    )


def decode_rle(rle: str, shape: tuple) -> np.ndarray:
    """Convert RLE to a binary mask; shape is (height, width)."""
    if len(shape) != 2 or any(
        not isinstance(n, (int, np.integer)) or n <= 0 for n in shape
    ):
        raise ValueError("shape must contain two positive integers")

    flat = np.zeros(int(shape[0]) * int(shape[1]), dtype=bool)
    values = [int(value) for value in rle.split()]
    if len(values) % 2:
        raise ValueError("RLE must contain start/length pairs")

    previous_end = 0
    for start, length in zip(values[::2], values[1::2]):
        end = start + length
        if start < previous_end or length <= 0 or end > flat.size:
            raise ValueError("Invalid, overlapping, unsorted, or out-of-bounds run")
        flat[start:end] = True
        previous_end = end

    return flat.reshape(shape, order="C")

A label map is a 2-D integer array: 0 is background, and each positive integer identifies one cell.

def labels_to_rles(label_map: np.ndarray, prefix: str) -> dict:
    """Convert positive instance labels to cell-ID/RLE entries."""
    label_map = np.asarray(label_map)
    if label_map.ndim != 2 or not np.issubdtype(label_map.dtype, np.integer):
        raise ValueError("label_map must be a 2-D integer array")
    if np.any(label_map < 0):
        raise ValueError("Use 0 for background and positive labels for cells")

    out = {}
    labels = np.unique(label_map[label_map > 0])
    for n, label in enumerate(labels, start=1):
        out[f"{prefix}_{n:06d}"] = encode_rle(label_map == label)
    return out

Use IVP and EXP as prefixes. IDs follow ascending label order; use the generated IDs in match_pairs.

4. Building submission.csv

Set the paths to your dataset. Test images are stored under hidden_test/<subject>/<region>/, and sample IDs use <subject>__<region>.

Pass your model function to build_submission. It must accept a region directory and return the in-vivo RLE dictionary, ex-vivo RLE dictionary, and match pairs.

import csv
import json
from pathlib import Path


def build_submission(predict_region, test_dir, sample_csv, output_csv):
    """Write one validated CSV row for every sample ID."""
    test_dir = Path(test_dir)
    sample_csv = Path(sample_csv)
    output_csv = Path(output_csv)
    columns = [
        "sample_id",
        "invivo_instances",
        "exvivo_instances",
        "match_pairs",
    ]

    with sample_csv.open("r", newline="", encoding="utf-8-sig") as file:
        reader = csv.DictReader(file)
        if reader.fieldnames != columns:
            raise ValueError("Unexpected sample submission columns")
        sample_ids = [row["sample_id"] for row in reader]

    if len(sample_ids) != 29 or len(set(sample_ids)) != 29:
        raise ValueError("Expected 29 unique sample IDs")

    rows = []
    for sample_id in sample_ids:
        subject, separator, region = sample_id.partition("__")
        if not separator or not subject or not region:
            raise ValueError(f"Unexpected sample ID: {sample_id}")
        region_dir = test_dir / subject / region
        for filename in ("invivo.tif", "exvivo.tif"):
            if not (region_dir / filename).is_file():
                raise FileNotFoundError(region_dir / filename)

        invivo, exvivo, pairs = predict_region(region_dir)
        for instances in (invivo, exvivo):
            if not isinstance(instances, dict) or not all(
                isinstance(cell_id, str) and isinstance(rle, str)
                for cell_id, rle in instances.items()
            ):
                raise ValueError("Instances must map string IDs to RLE strings")

        if not isinstance(pairs, list):
            raise ValueError("match_pairs must be a list")
        used_invivo, used_exvivo = set(), set()
        for pair in pairs:
            if (
                not isinstance(pair, (list, tuple))
                or len(pair) != 2
                or not all(isinstance(cell_id, str) for cell_id in pair)
            ):
                raise ValueError("Each match must contain two string cell IDs")
            iv_id, ex_id = pair
            if iv_id not in invivo or ex_id not in exvivo:
                raise ValueError(f"Match references an undefined cell: {pair}")
            if iv_id in used_invivo or ex_id in used_exvivo:
                raise ValueError(f"Cell appears in multiple matches: {pair}")
            used_invivo.add(iv_id)
            used_exvivo.add(ex_id)

        rows.append({
            "sample_id": sample_id,
            "invivo_instances": json.dumps(invivo),
            "exvivo_instances": json.dumps(exvivo),
            "match_pairs": json.dumps(pairs),
        })

    output_csv.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    with output_csv.open("w", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as file:
        writer = csv.DictWriter(file, fieldnames=columns)
        writer.writeheader()
        writer.writerows(rows)
    return output_csv


def empty_predictions(region_dir):

    predict_region=empty_predictions,

5. Submission Checklist

  • Exactly 29 data rows, with the four required columns and no extra index column.
  • Sample IDs match sample_submission.csv exactly, with no duplicates.
  • Instance fields are JSON objects; match pairs are lists of two IDs.
  • Matched IDs exist in the same row, and each cell appears in at most one pair.
  • RLE uses zero-based, row-major indexing, with valid runs inside the image.
  • Decoded masks match your predictions at the original image size.
  • Empty predictions use {} and [].
